assembly:
  - title: Find the premise
    text: Start with a what-if. Pin it down in one sentence, name the question the ending must answer, and decide what failure would cost.
    adds: [logline, dramatic-question, stakes, promise-payoff]
    makes: premise
  - title: Plan the story
    text: Grow the premise into people, a place and a chain of events. Put the scenes on cards and arrange them, as much or as little as suits you.
    adds: [premise, characters, setting, plot-structure, planning]
    makes: outline
  - title: Draft
    text: Choose who tells the story and how close we stand, then write it through to the end at a steady daily pace. Do not stop to polish.
    adds: [outline, narration, prose, drafting, daily-word-target]
    makes: first-draft
  - title: Revise
    text: Read the draft as a reader, test it on beta readers, find the theme you actually wrote, and cut it to length. Repeat until the big problems are gone.
    adds: [first-draft, revision-loop, reader-experience, theme, word-count]
    makes: revised-draft
  - title: Edit
    text: "Edit from big to small: structure, then sentences, then consistency, then typos. Format the result as a standard manuscript."
    adds: [revised-draft, editing-levels]
    makes: final-manuscript
  - title: Publish
    text: Choose a path to readers. The manuscript becomes a paperback, a hardback, an e-book or an audiobook.
    adds: [final-manuscript, publishing-path]
    makes: novel
---

A novel is a long invented story in prose, built to keep a reader turning pages. Writers think in layers: a premise with real stakes, characters who want things, a plot of scenes that turn on cause and effect, and narration and prose that control what the reader knows and feels. Under all of it sits the reader’s experience: questions opened, answers delayed, promises kept. Most of the work is a loop: plan, draft fast, then revise against how real readers respond. Structure models and rules of thumb are tools for diagnosing a draft, not forms to fill in.
